

Adaptive Heuristics for Order Batching Initiation Timing in Same-Day
## Delivery Warehouses

## Abstract
Same-day  delivery  (SDD)  intensifies  order  picking  and  order  batching  demands  in  e-commerce
distribution   warehouses,   yet   order   batching   initiation   timing   remains   critically   underexplored.
Traditional approaches — Fixed Time Window Batching (FTWB) and Variable Time Window Batching
(VTWB) — fail to exploit real-time system states, undermining order fulfilment performance. Grounded
in  Time-Based  Competition  (TBC)  theory,  this  study  develops  and  evaluates  six  adaptive  heuristics
incorporating order urgency and resource availability, benchmarked via discrete-event simulation across
6,912  scenarios  (207,360  runs).  The  Modified  Priority  Based  Heuristic  (MPBH)  achieves  the  greatest
reduction in total late orders, averaging 27.04% improvement over benchmarks. ANOVA reveals  that
operational context — trigger method, order rate, and picker availability — explains more performance
variance than heuristic choice alone. Crucially, batch initiation timing exerts greater influence on order
fulfilment than batch composition, extending TBC theory to warehouse micro-operations and yielding
computationally efficient, implementable heuristics..

Keywords: distribution warehouses; order fulfilment; order picking; order batching; same-day delivery;
time-based competition; adaptive heuristics; simulation.

## 1. Introduction
Rising consumer expectations for delivery velocity have established Same-Day Delivery (SDD) as
a critical competitive differentiator (Voccia et al., 2019), yet its operational execution presents substantial
logistical  complexities.  Within Time-Based  Competition (TBC)  theory (Stalk,  2012),  SDD  mandates
temporal efficiency—minimising order-to-fulfilment latency—over cost optimisation. Delays are costly,
reportedly diminishing sales by 8-12% (Niemi et al., 2020) and generating costs approximating 21% of
the order value (Vanheusden et al., 2022).
Adhering to SDD mandates depends critically on efficient warehouse operations, especially online
order  batching.  While  considerable  research  has  optimized  batch  composition (Van  Gils et  al.,  2018;
Cano et al., 2021) and picker routing (Jose Alejandro Cano, 2020; Bahçeci, Öncan, 2022), the pivotal
decision  of when to  initiate  batch  formation  in  real-time  remains  comparatively  underexplored.  This

timing  decision  embodies  a  critical  trade-off:  delaying  formation  for  larger,  more  efficient  batches
elevates the risk of failing to meet demanding delivery deadlines.
Conventional batch initiation timing relies on simplistic heuristics, primarily Fixed Time Window
Batching  (FTWB)  (fixed  intervals)  and  Variable  Time  Window  Batching  (VTWB)  (order  quantity
thresholds) (Gil-Borrás et  al.,  2020).  These  static  approaches  are  ill-suited  for  the  dynamic  milieu  of
SDD.  FTWB  cannot  adapt  to  fluctuating  order  rates,  while  VTWB,  though  responsive  to  volume,
neglects  critical  real-time  variables  like  order  urgency  and  resource  availability.  This  failure to  adapt
dynamically represents a significant gap in optimising time-sensitive SDD logistics.
This  research  confronts  this  deficiency  by  conceptualising  batch  initiation  timing  as  a  dynamic,
optimizable  decision  process.  Our  primary  research  question  is:  What  constitutes  an  optimal  timing
strategy  for  initiating  online  order  batching  to minimise total  late orders in  SDD  environments?
Secondary inquiries investigate the influence of operational factors on the relative performance of distinct
timing strategies.
We propose and rigorously evaluate a novel portfolio of adaptive timing heuristics that ascertain the
optimal  batching  moment  based  on  real-time  system  state  variables,  incorporating  calculated  order
urgency  metrics  and  resource  availability  assessments. A central feature is a proactive “urgent order”
prioritization  mechanism  that  estimates  expected  completion  times  relative  to  due  dates.  This  design
overcomes a key limitation of traditional methods, which typically incorporate due-date information only
after late   orders becomes   unavoidable. This   adaptive,   TBC-aligned   methodology   is   tested   by
benchmarking   six   new   strategies   (MaxPicker,   MaxCart,   UrgentPicker,   UrgentCart,   Simplified
Neighbourhood-based Heuristic (SNH), and Modified Priority Based Heuristic (MPBH)) against FTWB
and VTWB across a wide spectrum of simulated conditions.
This study offers three principal contributions. First, it advances batch initiation timing as a dynamic,
state-dependent decision, extending TBC theory to warehouse micro-operations. Second, it provides a
systematic comparative evaluation of novel adaptive timing heuristics across 6,912 simulated operational
scenarios, furnishing robust empirical evidence of their efficacy. Third, it introduces a tailored simulation
framework  for modelling SDD  dynamics.  This  framework  treats  time-sensitivity  as  a  primary
performance indicator. The subsequent sections of this paper detail the pertinent literature (Section 2),
delineate  the  methodology  and  simulation  framework  (Section  3),  describe  the  proposed  heuristics
(Section  4),  present  the  results  and  analysis  (Section  5),  and  discuss  the  implications,  limitations, and
avenues for future research (Section 6).
## 2. Literature Review

This review synthesizes research pertinent to optimising batch initiation timing in SDD warehouse
operations. We first establish the strategic context of SDD within TBC theory. We then critically examine
order  picking  and  batching  literature,  highlighting  how  prior  work,  despite  advancements,  has  largely
overlooked the dynamic optimisation of batch initiation timing. Finally, we consolidate these findings to
define the research gap and introduce this study's guiding propositions.
2.1 SDD and TBC
SDD intensifies the tension between escalating service level expectations and operational efficiency
(Chen et al., 2023). Within TBC theory, SDD prioritises temporal efficiency—compressing fulfilment
time—as  a  primary  competitive  advantage  over  cost minimisation (Voccia et  al.,  2019;  Klapp et  al.,
2020). This strategy is validated by consumer willingness to pay premiums for SDD (Chen et al., 2023)
and the correlation between on-time delivery and customer loyalty (Peng, Lu, 2017). However, reliable
SDD  performance  presents  distinct  operational  hurdles,  including  managing  small,  urgent  orders  and
ensuring tight integration with last-mile logistics and fixed dispatch schedules (Nesterova, Quak, 2016;
Klapp et al., 2018; Kuhn et al., 2021). This necessitates optimising internal warehouse processes, like
order  picking  and  batching,  within  a  TBC  framework  that  intrinsically  values  responsiveness. The
integration of warehouse timing decisions with last-mile delivery constraints is increasingly recognised
as a hierarchical optimisation challenge in e-commerce fulfilment, where dispatch sequencing directly
shapes downstream service outcomes (Babaei et al., 2023).
2.2 Order Picking and Batching Optimisation Landscape
Order picking is the most labour-intensive warehouse function, reportedly representing up to 55%
of  total  operational  expenses (de  Koster et  al.,  2007).  E-commerce  SDD  exacerbates  this  with  high
volumes  of  small,  unpredictable  orders,  wide  item  dispersion,  and  constrained  processing  windows
(Klapp et al., 2018). Order batching, grouping orders into consolidated tasks, is a key strategy to enhance
picking  efficiency  and  throughput (Henn,  Schmid,  2013).  Research  distinguishes  between  offline
batching (orders known a priori) and online batching (orders arrive dynamically), the latter characterising
SDD environments (Zhang et al., 2020).

Early batching research focused on minimising picker travel distance (Keshavarz et al., 2021), but
later studies incorporated tardiness minimisation as a key objective (Azadnia et al., 2013; Henn, 2015).
Significant  advancements  occurred  in  optimising  batch  composition  (which  orders  to  group)  and
sequencing (processing order). For instance, Henn and Schmid (2013) used Iterated Local Search (ILS)

and  Ant-Based  Hyper-heuristic  (ABHC)  heuristics  for  the  Order  Batching  and  Sequencing  Problem
(OBSP) to minimise tardiness. Others tackled integrated OBSP and Routing problems (Gil-Borrás et al.,
2021). Sophisticated metaheuristics like General Variable Neighbourhood Search (GVNS) and Variable
Neighbourhood  Search/Descent  (VNS/VND)  have  shown  considerable  tardiness  reductions  in  offline
settings (Henn, 2012; Bustillo et al., 2015; Menéndez et al., 2017; Scholz et al., 2017).
However,   even   studies   explicitly   targeting   tardiness   often   operate   under   implicit   or   static
assumptions  regarding  when  the  batching  process  is  initiated.  While  optimising  batch  content  and
sequence  after  a  trigger  occurs  is  crucial,  the  timing  of  that  trigger  fundamentally  constrains  overall
performance potential. If the trigger is unresponsive to real-time conditions, even optimally composed
batches may be formed too late to meet deadlines.
More recently, research has addressed the problem's inherently online nature, frequently leveraging
machine learning. Cals et al. (2021) and Beeks et al. (2022) utilised Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
for dynamic batching decisions, while Shavaki and Jolai (2021) proposed integrated models for online
batching  and  delivery  planning.  Although  this  is  significant  progress,  their  primary  focus  remains  on
assigning  incoming  orders  to  evolving  batches  or  determining  processing  sequences.  Their  state
representations and action spaces may not fully capture or prioritize the variables most pertinent to the
batch  initiation  timing  decision  itself.  Consequently,  their  timing  mechanisms  often  rely  on  implicit
thresholds rather than treating the 'when' decision as a distinct, optimizable process.
Therefore, despite considerable sophistication in determining optimal batch content, sequence, and
routing once a batching decision is initiated, the critical antecedent question of when to optimally trigger
this  process  in  response  to  dynamically  fluctuating  system  conditions  (e.g.,  order  urgency,  resource
availability) remains largely underdeveloped.
2.3 Timing Decisions in Online Order Batching
The  decision  of  when  to  initiate  batch  formation  is  fundamental  in online and time-critical  SDD
operations. It encapsulates the core operational trade-off: rapid initiation risks inefficiently small batches,
whereas delaying initiation to accumulate more orders increases the probability of orders becoming late.
Effective  timing  mechanisms  must  be  sensitive  to  the  current  operational  context.  Despite  its
acknowledged importance, initiation timing is often governed by simple,  non-adaptive rules. Schleyer
and Gue (2012),  for  instance,  modeled  picking  times  but  evaluated  them  under  a  fixed  time  window
policy,  without optimising the  timing  decision  itself.  The  most  common methods,  FTWB  and  VTWB
(Gil-Borrás et al., 2020), are non-adaptive. This timing challenge shares conceptual parallels with task
dispatching  decisions  in  production  systems,  where  the  choice  of  initiation  rule  has  been  shown  to

significantly  determine  tardiness  outcomes  across  varying  operational  conditions—underscoring  the
generality  of  timing  as  a  performance-critical  decision  variable (Lian et  al.,  2022). Their  rigidity  and
reliance  on  simple  thresholds  (time  or  order  count)  ignore  real-time  order  urgency  and  resource
availability, representing a critical failure to leverage temporal efficiencies in a TBC context.
2.4 Research Gap and Propositions
This  critical  review  reveals  a  significant  research  gap:  the  deficiency  of  research  developing  and
evaluating adaptive, state-dependent strategies for dynamically determining when to initiate batching in
online and time-sensitive  order fulfilment systems  like  SDD.  While  substantial  scholarly  effort  has
focused on optimising processes after the batching trigger (i.e., composition, sequencing, routing), the
triggering mechanism itself has largely remained  static or based on simplistic rules, failing to harness
real-time system information effectively for improved temporal performance.
Based on this identified gap and framed within TBC theory, this study investigates the potential of
adaptive timing heuristics. We formulate the following research propositions to guide our inquiry:
Proposition 1: Adaptive heuristics for batch initiation timing, which incorporate real-time system state
information  (e.g.,  order  urgency,  resource  availability),  will  significantly  outperform
traditional  static  timing  rules  (FTWB,  VTWB)  in minimising total  late orders within
dynamic SDD environments.
Proposition 2: Resource-aware  adaptive  heuristics  will  demonstrate  greater  robustness  (i.e.,  more
consistent performance) across variations in operational conditions, such as fluctuating
order arrival rates and picker availability, compared to traditional timing approaches.
Proposition 3: The relative performance ranking of different batch initiation timing strategies will vary
systematically depending on the specific operational context, defined by factors such as
picker availability, cart capacity constraints, and the routing policies employed.
Proposition 4: In time-critical SDD environments, the strategic timing of batch initiation may have a
comparable  or  potentially  greater  impact  on  overall  system  performance  (particularly
total late orders) than the specific method used for subsequent batch composition (e.g.,
First-Come-First-Served vs. Earliest Due Date).
These  propositions  directly  emanate  from  the  limitations  identified  in  the  existing  literature.
Proposition 1 targets the primary gap concerning the paucity of adaptive timing mechanisms. Proposition
2 addresses the insufficient analysis of robustness in dynamic settings. Proposition 3 confronts the need
for understanding context-dependent performance, moving beyond generalized findings. Proposition 4

critically   re-evaluates   the   relative   importance   of   timing   versus   subsequent   batching   decisions,
challenging the predominant focus observed in much of the batching literature.

3.2 Warehouse Model and Operational Environment
A discrete-event simulation models a manual picker-to-part distribution warehouse that is similar to
the one seen in Ho et al. (2008). As shown in Figure 1, the warehouse has twelve parallel picking aisles
and two cross aisles (one front cross aisle and one back cross aisle). It also has an  I/O point at one of its
corners. The I/O point is not only the place where pickers receive order-picking instructions, but also the place
where pickers deposit the items they have picked from the warehouse. Key assumptions include: ABC storage
(approximating 80/20 rule, (Silva et al., 2022)); homogeneous Poisson order arrivals (λ) (Boysen et al.,
2019); order sizes of 1-10 items (discrete uniform); and SDD-reflective due times (30-120 minutes post-
arrival, discrete uniform, (Voccia et al., 2019)). Manual pickers operate at 0.5 m/s walking speed with a
5s  item  pick  time (Burinskienė, Lerher, 2021),  using  S-shape  or  Largest  Gap  routing (Roodbergen,
Koster,  2001).  These  parameters,  while  acknowledging  complexities  like non-Poisson  arrivals,  ensure
controlled experimentation and comparability.

In our model, 푆푒푡푢푝푇푖푚푒 is defined as the fixed, non-divisible time required for all preparatory and
concluding  tasks  for  a  single  picking  tour  (e.g.,  administrative  tasks,  equipment  preparation,  and  time
allowance), as established in foundational warehouse optimisation literature (Henn, 2012; Coruzzolo et
al.,  2023).  For  this  simulation,  this  value  was  set  to  a  constant  30  seconds,  an  empirically-derived
estimate representing the average time for a picker to receive a mission, acquire a cart, and travel to the
first 'A' zone pick location.
The inclusion of this per-batch cost (Yu, 2008) in a per-order urgency check is not an accounting
error,  but  a  critical  and  deliberate  feature  of  the  predictive  model.  The  purpose  of  the  heuristic  is  to
estimate if an order is at risk of becoming late. To do this, it must model the 푇표푡푎푙퐵푎푡푐ℎ푇푖푚푒 of the
most conservative, "worst-case" scenario, which is a single-order batch.
As established by the core equations for batch completion time (Yu, 2008; Coruzzolo et al., 2023),
the total service time for any batch, regardless of size, is 푇표푡푎푙퐵푎푡푐ℎ푇푖푚푒 = 푃푟표푐푒푠푠푖푛푔푇푖푚푒(표) +
푆푒푡푢푝푇푖푚푒. Therefore, to accurately predict the completion time of a potential single-order batch, the
model must include the fixed 푆푒푡푢푝푇푖푚푒 cost that this tour would incur. Omitting 푆푒푡푢푝푇푖푚푒  would
be a methodological error, as it would  falsely  assume a  zero-cost batch initiation, which the literature
proves is a primary source of cascading delays (Gil-Borrás et al., 2020). This calculation does not "add"
푆푒푡푢푝푇푖푚푒 to  every  order;  it  correctly  models  the 푇표푡푎푙퐵푎푡푐ℎ푇푖푚푒 for  the  specific,  potential  batch
being evaluated (a batch of size 1) to determine if that order is a time-critical risk.
The 푇푟푎푣푒푙푇푖푚푒(퐼푡푒푚푠(표)) term  in  the 푃푟표푐푒푠푠푖푛푔푇푖푚푒(표) formula  is  not  derived  from  a  full
path  calculation,  which  would  be  computationally  prohibitive  in  real-time.  Instead,  it  is  an  O(1)
complexity approximation using a regression-based formula derived from the number of items and their
likely ABC zone distribution.
3.3 Experimental Design, Simulation, and Validation
A full factorial experimental design evaluated strategies under diverse conditions. Factors included:
Trigger Method (8 levels: FTWB, VTWB benchmarks, and 6 novel heuristics) (Gil-Borrás et al., 2020);
Incoming Order Rate (λ) (6 levels: 0.5 to 16 orders/min); Number of Pickers (12 levels: 1 to 12) (Scholz
et al., 2017); Batching Method (2 levels: First-Come-First-Served, FCFS; or Earliest Due Date, EDT)
(Henn, 2015); Cart Capacity (C) (3 levels: 100, 150, 200 items) (Van Gils et al., 2018; Vanheusden et

al.,  2022);  and  Routing  Policy  (2  levels:  S-shape  or  Largest  Gap) (Roodbergen,  Koster,  2001).  This
design yielded 6,912 unique scenarios (8x6x12x2x3x2), each replicated 30 times (207,360 total runs) for
statistical  robustness. This  parametric,  large-scale  simulation  design  mirrors  recent  computational
experiments in the dispatching literature that systematically evaluate multiple rules across varying load
and variability conditions to identify context-dependent performance differences (Castro et al., 2024).
The  simulation  was  built  in  Python  (SimPy), modelling an  8-hour  day  (28,800s)  with  a  warm-up
period determined by Welch's method. The model manages order generation, pooling, batch triggering,
FCFS/EDT batch formation (respecting capacity), picker assignment (longest idle time first) (Ardjmand
et  al.,  2018),  and routing. The  trigger  serves  only  to  initiate  a  batching  cycle.  Once  activated,  batch
formation continues using the selected batching rule until either the order pool is empty or no picker is
available. The trigger condition is not re-applied within an active cycle. Rigorous three-phase validation
was performed: (1) Conceptual Validation (domain experts); (2) Computational Verification (systematic
code  testing);  and  (3)  Operational  Validation  (comparing  outputs  to  known  results,  checking  face
validity, and sensitivity analyses).

- Discussion and Conclusion
This study demonstrates that adaptive batch initiation timing, informed by real-time order urgency
and resource availability, is a critical component of SDD warehousing.
The central finding is not simply that one heuristic is universally superior, but that the context of the
operation dictates heuristic performance. The significant three-way interaction effect between the trigger
method, incoming rate, picker availability (η
p
² = 0.396) explained vastly more variance in total late orders
than the main effect of the Trigger Method alone (η
p
² = 0.0168). This confirms that the primary challenge
is selecting a heuristic logic that is robust to the specific operational environment (Proposition 3).
Within this context-dependent landscape, the MPBH emerged as the most robust strategy, achieving
an average 27.04% total late orders reduction over static benchmarks (Proposition 1). Its superiority was
most pronounced in high-load scenarios (Figure 14). This robustness stems from its iterative logic: unlike
simpler heuristics, MPBH actively balances urgency (sorting by due date) against constraints (capacity

and  predicted total  late  orders).  By  removing  the  latest  due  date  order  upon  constraint  violation,  it
dynamically prioritises the most time-critical orders while still attempting to build efficient batches.
Our findings extend prior work that often treats timing as a static parameter (Schleyer, Gue, 2012)
showing that optimising the trigger itself yields substantial performance gains. This result is consistent
with emerging evidence that frameworks combining adaptive heuristics with stochastic simulation yield
substantial advantages over fixed-parameter approaches in on-demand delivery settings, where real-time
variability  renders  static  rules  structurally  insufficient (Zhang et  al.,  2025). This  addresses  FTWB
limitations  in  volatile  SDD  environments (Voccia et  al.,  2019) by  leveraging  TBC  principles (Stalk,
2012) to balance batching efficiency against delivery risk.
The  success  of  urgency-aware  heuristics  (MPBH,  UrgentPicker)  supports  prior  emphasis  on  due-
date  orientation (Henn,  2015).  Unlike  offline  metaheuristics  such  as  GVNS (Bustillo et  al.,  2015;
Menéndez et al., 2017), our heuristics are computationally efficient (O(n) to O(n log n)) and designed
for real-time online decision-making in dynamic SDD operations. Notably, MPBH’s structured, state-
dependent  logic  offers  an  implementable  solution  that  contrasts  with  more  opaque,  black-box  DRL
approaches (Cals et al., 2021; Beeks et al., 2022).
The  context-dependency  of  heuristic  performance  (Proposition  3)  is  consistent  with  findings  in
related logistics problems (Dauod, Won, 2022). Proposition 2 (robustness) is only partially supported;
the dramatic failure of 'UrgentCart' (Section 5.3) highlights that adaptivity does not guarantee stability,
a critical nuance for heuristic design.
Perhaps the most significant finding, strongly supporting Proposition 4, is that the timing of batch
initiation has a greater impact on total late orders than the method of batch composition (FCFS vs. EDT).
The timing heuristic explained substantially more variance in total late orders (main effect η
p
## ² = 0.0168)
than composition (η
p
² = 0.000014). This suggests a paradigm shift: in time-critical SDD, "when" to batch
may matter more than "how" (Shavaki, Jolai, 2021). We must state this finding with a critical caveat:
this comparison is limited to the simple, non-advanced composition rules (FCFS/EDT) used in this study.
Whether   this   finding   holds   when   compared   against   sophisticated,   proximity-based   composition
metaheuristics (e.g., Henn & Schmid, 2013) remains a critical question for future research.
6.1 Limitations and Model Simplifications
While  this  simulation  study  is  extensive,  its  external  validity  is  bounded  by  two  key modelling
simplifications.  First,  the  model  employs  a  homogeneous  Poisson  process  (HPP)  for  order  arrivals
(Boysen et al., 2019), which does not capture the non-stationary nature of a true SDD environment that
features distinct peaks and lulls (i.e., a non-homogeneous Poisson process, or NHPP). We hypothesise

that an NHPP would, in fact, amplify the performance differences, as the adaptive rules (like MPBH) are
specifically  designed  to  respond  to  such  volatility,  while  static  rules  (FTWB)  would  fail  significantly
during unmanaged peaks.
Second, the model does not include picker congestion or blocking. In a high-load scenario (e.g., 12
pickers  at  16  orders/min),  congestion  would  become  a  dominant  factor  in  operational  delays,  and  our
processing   time   estimates   are   therefore   optimistic.   We   hypothesise   that   congestion   would
disproportionately degrade the performance of simple, resource-aware heuristics like 'MaxPicker'. This
rule triggers immediately when a picker is free,  which, in a congested system, would merely dispatch
more pickers into an already-crowded environment, potentially worsening the bottleneck. The logic of
MPBH, which balances urgency against batch capacity, may be inherently more robust to these effects,
though this remains to be proven.
6.2 Implications and Future Research
Theoretically, this work advances the order batching literature by elevating batch initiation timing
from  an  implicit  parameter  to  a  critical,  state-dependent  decision  variable,  extending  TBC  theory  to
warehouse  micro-operations (Peng,  Lu,  2017).  Practically,  strategies  like  MPBH  are  implementable
using  standard  WMS  data  (order  pool,  due  times,  picker  status)  with  low  computational  overhead
(ranging  from O(n) for  urgency-aware  rules  to O(n  log  n) for  MPBH).  The  27.04%  average total  late
orders reduction suggests significant potential for cost savings and customer satisfaction improvement.
Based on our findings and these limitations, future research should move in several directions. The
most  pressing  need  is  to  evaluate  these  heuristics  under  more  realistic  conditions,  including  empirical
non-homogeneous  arrival  patterns  (NHPP)  and  by  incorporating  dynamic  picker  congestion  models.
Furthermore, to truly understand the optimality gap, MPBH should be benchmarked against (a) optimal
solutions for small, tractable instances (e.g., via Mixed-Integer Programming, MIP) and (b) state-of-the-
art dynamic scheduling rules from the literature, not just simple composition methods. Finally, this work
should be extended by integrating warehouse timing decisions with last-mile delivery constraints, such
as fixed vehicle dispatch schedules. The integrated scheduling-routing framework for products with hard
delivery   deadlines—where   time   constraints   function   analogously   to   SDD   cutoffs—offers   a
methodological template for such extensions (Dantas et al., 2024).
